The Gaussian Is Enough:
Flow-Matching Priors Do Not Help

Key Finding

Non-Gaussian action priors that are measurably closer to the target data help when training an imitation learning policy from scratch. We do not observe this benefit when fine-tuning a pretrained Large Behavior Model (LBM). Our diagnostic analyses indicate that the observation encoder, not the action prior, governs fine-tuning performance.

Across over 100K simulated rollouts on three LBM architectures (LBM 1.0, π0.5, and GR00T N1.5) and 40+ tasks, and 1250 hardware rollouts on five bimanual tasks, the standard Gaussian prior performs statistically indistinguishably from, or better than, closer-to-target action priors.

Five LBM Eval tasks, 1000 rollouts per prior. Left: ℓ2 distance between prior samples and ground-truth actions; Apre is 3× closer to the target than the Gaussian. Middle: when training from scratch, Apre significantly outperforms the Gaussian. Right: after fine-tuning from the same pretrained LBM 1.0 checkpoint, the Gaussian instead significantly outperforms Apre.

Abstract

Modern robot imitation learning increasingly relies on generative policies based on diffusion or flow-matching models, which generate actions by transforming samples from a prior distribution. A key question is whether the choice of prior matters. Replacing the standard Gaussian with a closer-to-target, non-Gaussian prior has been shown to substantially improve performance when training from scratch.

A natural next step is to ask whether these gains transfer to fine-tuning pretrained Large Behavior Models (LBMs) such as LBM 1.0, π0.5, and GR00T N1.5. Surprisingly, we find that this is not the case, except possibly at very low fine-tuning data fractions. Across over 100K simulation rollouts and 1250 hardware rollouts, non-Gaussian priors that are demonstrably closer to the target yield statistically indistinguishable or worse fine-tuning performance than a standard Gaussian.

Diagnostic analyses suggest why: fine-tuned policies converge to similar action predictions across priors, despite their encoder embeddings diverging substantially. A learning-rate ablation confirms that encoder training is the dominant factor, substantially outweighing the effect of prior choice.

What We Compared

We compare seven action priors: the standard Gaussian, three that we construct directly from the pretrained LBM with no auxiliary training, and three baselines from the literature. Within each experiment, the task, data, architecture, optimizer, and number of fine-tuning steps are held identical across priors; only the prior differs.

Our study is guided by three research questions:

  • Q1. Do non-Gaussian priors that are measurably closer to the target improve policy fine-tuning performance?
  • Q2. Do informed priors have an advantage across data fractions, particularly in the low-data regime, where the added inductive bias may be most beneficial?
  • Q3. Is the effect of prior choice consistent across model architectures, and across simulation and hardware?

Four experimental settings address these questions:

  • (1) Controlled test (Q1): a prior constructed to be measurably close to the target, using LBM 1.0 on five LBM Eval tasks in simulation (14,000 rollouts).
  • (2) Full-scale fine-tuning in simulation (Q1, Q2): LBM 1.0 on eight LBM Eval tasks unseen during pretraining, at five fine-tuning data fractions (56,000 rollouts).
  • (3) Bimanual hardware (Q3): blind A/B testing on five bimanual tasks with two Franka FR3 arms (1250 rollouts).
  • (4) Other LBM architectures (Q3): π0.5 and GR00T N1.5 on 34 RoboCasa365 tasks (54,400 rollouts).

How to read the plots. Each violin shows the posterior distribution of one prior's success rate. The letters form a compact letter display (CLD) computed from a sequential hypothesis test at α = 0.05 with Bonferroni correction: priors that share a letter are not statistically separable; priors with no letter in common differ significantly.

Results

(1) Controlled test: a closer-to-target prior helps from scratch, but not after fine-tuning (Q1)

To control the quality of the prior, we first fine-tune LBM 1.0 with the Gaussian prior on 50% of the demonstrations, producing a task-aware checkpoint whose actions are measurably closer to the target than Gaussian samples. We then fine-tune again from this checkpoint, varying only the prior. When training from scratch, Apre significantly outperforms the Gaussian (roughly 28% vs. 18% success). After fine-tuning, no prior is statistically separable from the Gaussian.

(2) Full-scale fine-tuning in simulation: priors are statistically tied at every data fraction above 5% (Q1, Q2)

We fine-tune LBM 1.0 on eight LBM Eval tasks unseen during pretraining, using 5%, 25%, 50%, 75%, and 100% of the available demonstrations. From 25% upward, all seven priors share CLD group "a". The one exception is the 5% fraction (approximately 10 to 20 demonstrations per task), where Epre+σE𝒵 significantly outperforms the Gaussian (25.9% vs. 18.9%), suggesting that the embedding-space perturbation acts as a useful inductive bias when demonstrations are very scarce.

Qualitatively, the fine-tuned policies behave similarly. Below, the seven policies fine-tuned on 100% of the DumpVegetablesIntoContainer demonstrations are rolled out from an identical initial condition. Successful rollouts are nearly indistinguishable across priors; failed rollouts exhibit prior-specific failure modes.

(3) Bimanual hardware: the finding holds on real robots (Q3)

We fine-tune LBM 1.0 on five bimanual tasks on two Franka FR3 arms and evaluate five priors with blind A/B testing: 50 rollouts per prior per task, 1250 in total. Four of the five priors fall in the same CLD group on both success rate and partial task progress. BRIDGER is the sole outlier, consistent with the instability we observed in its CVAE training.

All hardware rollouts for two of the five tasks, each followed by a zoom-in on a single initial condition. In the full grids, each row is one prior and each tile one rollout (50 per prior), time-compressed to 10 s and tagged S (success, green) or F (failure, red).

On BikeRotorInstall, success rates are low (18% for the Gaussian, 0 of 50 for BRIDGER), so no initial condition exists on which all five policies succeed. The row below shows an initial condition on which all five fail while still making comparable partial progress: every policy picks up the rotor and seats it on the hub, then diverges. Gaussian 𝒵 and Retrieval skip the lockring and reach for the tool; Epre+σE𝒵, Cocos, and BRIDGER place the lockring and pick up the tool but drop it between the spokes.

On FoodBank, the same pattern as in simulation appears: at one initial condition all five fine-tuned policies succeed with similar motions; at another, all five fail, each with a different failure mode.

(4) Other LBM architectures: the finding holds on π0.5 and GR00T N1.5 (Q3)

We repeat the comparison on the RoboCasa365 benchmark with two additional LBMs, π0.5 and GR00T N1.5, each fine-tuned on 34 tasks. On both architectures, the Gaussian and Apre+σA𝒵 tie in the top CLD group, while Apre is significantly worse despite remaining roughly 3× closer to the target in ℓ2. Both models were pretrained predominantly on real-world data, so actions from a frozen copy of the pretrained model are out of distribution for the simulated demonstrations; priors regularized by Gaussian noise appear less affected by this domain gap.

Why Does the Action Prior Not Matter Under Fine-Tuning?

In short: different action priors lead to different fine-tuned encoder representations, but these representations are equally predictive of the target actions. Once the encoder is sufficiently well pretrained, it governs policy performance; the choice of action prior does not.

(1) Fine-tuned encoders are equally predictive of the target actions

LogME is a training-free estimate of how well an encoder's features predict the target actions. LogME scores are statistically inseparable across priors at every data fraction: the prior used during fine-tuning leaves no detectable effect on the quality of the fine-tuned encoder's features.

(2) Encoder representations diverge, action predictions do not

Relative to the pretrained model, the fine-tuned action predictions change by a comparable amount across priors. The encoder embeddings, however, diverge sharply: the Gaussian moves the encoder least from its pretrained state, while Apre induces roughly 2 to 2.5× the change.

(3) Encoder training matters more than prior choice

Reducing the observation encoder's learning rate to 1/10 of the action head's lowers success rate by 15 to 17 percentage points for every prior in simulation, and by 9 points on hardware. No change of prior produces an effect of comparable magnitude.
